Yes to all of this. As agents take on more and more long running project work, and you have swarms of agents that parallelize work against a goal, the agent harness and architecture for orchestration will have to evolve. One of the biggest shifts is how those agents collectively share their knowledge, keep track of the tasks, and also coordinate with human users. This in theory is technically straightforward, but there’s a ton of complexity to get right to ensure agents can both get access to the revenant resources without too much or little exposure. There are a lot of lessons to pull from based on how people already do this kind of work together (people share their files, have computers to work with, community in chats and inboxes, and so on). But equally the primitives need to be able to enable this work at a throughput far higher than people, and in a world where agents can much more easily become a vector of security risk. Going to be super fun building out this next generation of how agents operate. Lots of opportunity here both for horizontal and vertical specific approaches to bring this to life. This post nails why we why we should take the Riemann Hypothesis breakthrough very seriously, even beyond math. We’re entering an era where solving problems will look very different from how we solved them in the past. “This is not theory-building in the usual sense. It is something much weirder and more alien. The best human mathematicians know how to build theory, painstakingly, to reach a goal. But no human brain, on its own, is capable of creating a wormhole that requires a 25,000 page long tour through existing infrastructure with no obvious or realistic connection to the goal. But huge credit to Levent here: I think no current AI, on its own, is capable of this either.” It used to be the case that to solve a particular problem - code, life sciences, math, cyber, engineering, and so on - you were ultimately bound by how much information an individual or team could retain and understand about the space. More information or more team members don’t always help, because it gets harder to connect the dots or coordinate amongst everyone. AI changes that completely. For the first time ever we can decouple the human context limits and ability to work through information from our ability to drive productivity or discoveries. This is likely not just a small increase in the kinds of discoveries we can drive, but a qualitative shift in human output. Now every single form of prior art, insight, past research, past decision, or unrelated breakthrough, can be used for the discovery or creation of something new. This is useful for anything from cybersecurity to life sciences research. Agents will be able to correlate entirely unrelated work to a separate project; scan every bit of data in your enterprise for solving a problem; and so on. The solutions may not be as elegant as they were in the past, but we sure will have far more of them. We’re only in the first couple of months of actually understanding the implications of this but it’s clearly going to drive a lot of change. Going to be a big day in math https://t.co/v0sKYQLWSB https://t.co/dclzIoOPj1 This year we’ve had a clear jump in model capability where instead of telling agents precisely what to do, it’s better to use these systems to solve problems you can’t easily define yourself. A year ago you’d need to very clearly tell agents the how to design and develop your product idea, or there was almost no chance of success. Now, increasingly, the agent likely can come up with a better implementation than you’d have thought of because you can have it connect the dots between a far greater solution space than you can. This will be increasingly true in basically every domain of kneeler work. We’re going to see this impact in finance, legal, life sciences, cyber, and many other spaces where no person can hold onto all the available relevant industry knowledge to think of the best solution. Ultimately this is going to reshape our relationship with AI and how we deploy agents in our work. It won’t be about explicitly telling them, but instead giving them goals and enough freedom to pursue them in the best way they can.
